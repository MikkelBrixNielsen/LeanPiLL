\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
This section reflects on the design choices made throughout the formalisation, discusses alternatives explored during development, and identifies limitations of the resulting Lean development.

\subsection{Proof architecture}
The architecture of the transition semantics was revised during development. An earlier formulation did not introduce a transition system over typing derivations and instead attempted to relate process transitions directly to environment transitions. Under this approach, the typability proof relied on inversion of environment transitions to recover the hyperenvironment structure needed to reconstruct a typing derivation after a process step. Consequently, the environment transition relation accumulated additional freshness and structural premises whose purpose was to support the metatheoretic proof rather than to describe the resource-level transition itself.

This approach was abandoned because it obscured the intended role of the environment semantics and diverged from the derivation-level organisation of the paper presentation. In the current formulation, \texttt{EnvStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ contains only the premises required to describe valid environment-level transitions. The stronger freshness, linearity, and component-preservation information required for reconstruction is instead carried by \texttt{TypingStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ and its auxiliary invariants. The theorem \path{session_fidelity_env}$_{\texttt{m}}$ then establishes the forward projection from typed transitions to environment transitions.

\subsection{Proof-engineering trade-offs}
Several premises in the typing and transition relations are included for proof convenience rather than logical necessity. The most prominent example is the post-state disjointness proof \texttt{hD2} in the parallel constructors \texttt{par}$_1$ and \texttt{par}$_2$ of \texttt{TypingStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$. The former is shown in \cref{lst:typingstep-par1}. This premise is derivable from the pre-state disjointness proof \texttt{hD1}, the freshness condition requiring the names introduced by the label to be disjoint from the non-stepping component, and the structural property that transitions introduce only names recorded by their labels. Keeping \texttt{hD2} explicit avoids reconstructing this derived fact in every inductive case, at the cost of slightly cluttering the inductive definition. This is representative of a broader proof-engineering trade-off: some redundancy in the definitions can make individual proofs shorter and more robust, even when it makes the relations less minimal.

A second source of proof overhead is the mismatch between concrete freshness witnesses in \texttt{ProcStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ and co-finite premises in the typing rules. The binding constructors of \texttt{ProcStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ record the particular names introduced by a transition, whereas the corresponding typing rules are stated co-finitely. The simulation proofs must therefore show that the co-finite typing premise can be instantiated with the concrete names chosen by the process transition. This is handled by auxiliary \emph{all-fresh} lemmas, such as those for the \texttt{tensor} and \texttt{parr} prefix binders. Thus, the current architecture keeps the process transition relation concrete and structurally direct, at the cost of additional freshness-bridging infrastructure in the simulation proofs.

\paragraph{Summary of shifted proof obligations}
The proof effort exposed by the mechanisation is concentrated less in transcribing the paper rules than in establishing the infrastructure required to use those rules inductively. This shift arises from deliberate choices in the representation and semantic architecture of the formalisation. The locally nameless representation avoids reasoning directly up to $\alpha$-equivalence, co-finite typing rules avoid fixing particular fresh representatives, and list-based environments provide a simple account of structural composition. These choices are accompanied by proof obligations concerning opening, freshness transfer, permutation, and reconstruction. These trade-offs show that mechanisation does not merely replace informal arguments with automation. Instead, it makes explicit the auxiliary infrastructure required by the chosen representation and semantic architecture. A separate limitation, considered next, is that the formalisation does not currently include the process-level structural congruence.

\subsection{Structural congruence}
The current formalisation does not include a structural congruence relation for processes. In the paper presentation of \pill{}, structural congruence identifies processes modulo the commutative, associative, and unit laws of parallel composition, together with scope manipulation for restriction. The mechanisation recovers the typing-level counterpart of the parallel-composition laws through the monoidal laws for hyperenvironments and the exchange rules of the typing relation. However, these results do not establish corresponding equivalences between process terms. For example, while an empty hyperenvironment component can be removed from the typing structure, the formalisation does not identify $P \pp_p \nil$ with $P$ at the process level.

This omission matters because the operational semantics is defined over concrete process syntax rather than over processes modulo structural congruence. Although the transition rules propagate actions through parallel contexts, they do not identify syntactically different but structurally equivalent process terms. Consequently, intermediate processes may retain parallel units or a particular association and ordering of parallel components even when these distinctions are immaterial in the paper presentation. Moreover, the process semantics does not currently support the scope-manipulation principles for restriction supplied by structural congruence in the paper. The current development has not shown structural congruence to be necessary for the proofs established so far, but its omission limits the correspondence between the mechanised process semantics and the structural flexibility of the paper presentation.

\subsection{Current limitations}
The static formalisation covers the full \pill{} calculus, whereas the operational semantics and metatheory currently target only \pimll{}. Even within this fragment, session fidelity is not yet fully mechanised: the remaining incomplete case of \path{typability_subject_reduction}$_{\texttt{m}}$ is the \texttt{res} case, where the post-transition structure required to reconstruct the enclosing \texttt{cut} derivation has not yet been recovered. Thus, the present mechanisation establishes the central preservation argument for the multiplicative fragment except for the \texttt{res} case.

The formalisation also does not yet cover several further results from the paper metatheory. In particular, the behavioural theory of similarity, bisimilarity, and congruence remains unmechanised, as do the diamond property, serialisation, non-interference, readiness, and the associated productivity result. Addressing these limitations requires both completing the remaining metatheoretic proof obligations for \pimll{} and extending the mechanised operational and behavioural theory towards the full calculus. These extensions are considered in the following chapter.
