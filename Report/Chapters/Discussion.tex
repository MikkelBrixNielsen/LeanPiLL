\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
This section reflects on the design choices made throughout the formalisation, discusses alternatives that were explored, and identifies limitations of the resulting Lean development.

\subsection{Proof Architecture}
The role of \texttt{EnvStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ was refined during development. An earlier formulation did not introduce a transition system over typing derivations and instead attempted to relate process transitions directly to environment transitions. In this approach, the typability proof relied on inversion of environment transitions to recover the hyperenvironment structure required for reconstruction. As a result, the environment relation accumulated additional freshness and structural premises whose purpose was to support the proof rather than to describe the resource-level transition itself.

This approach was abandoned because it obscured the intended role of the environment semantics and diverged from the derivation-level organisation of the paper presentation. In the current formulation, \texttt{EnvStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ retains only the premises required to describe valid environment-level transitions, while the stronger freshness, linearity, and component-preservation information needed for reconstruction is carried by \texttt{TypingStep}$_{\texttt{m}}$ and its auxiliary invariants. The theorem \path{session_fidelity_env}$_{\texttt{m}}$ then establishes the forward projection from typed transitions to environment transitions.

\subsection{Proof-engineering trade-offs}
Several premises in the typing and transition relations are included for proof convenience rather than logical necessity. The most prominent example is the post-state disjointness proof \texttt{hD2} in the parallel constructors \texttt{par$_1$} and \texttt{par$_2$} of \texttt{TypingStep}$_\texttt{m}$. The former is shown in \cref{lst:typingstep-par1}. This premise is derivable from the pre-state disjointness proof \texttt{hD1}, the freshness condition requiring the names introduced by the label to be disjoint from the non-stepping component, and the structural property that transitions introduce only names recorded by their labels. Keeping \texttt{hD2} explicit avoids reconstructing this derived fact in every inductive case, at the cost of slightly cluttering the inductive definition. This is representative of a broader proof-engineering trade-off: some redundancy in the definitions can make individual proofs shorter and more robust, even when it makes the relations less minimal.

A second source of proof overhead is the mismatch between concrete freshness witnesses in \texttt{ProcStep}$_\texttt{m}$ and co-finite premises in the typing rules. The binding constructors of \texttt{ProcStep}$_\texttt{m}$ record the particular names introduced by a transition, whereas the corresponding typing rules are stated co-finitely. The simulation proofs must therefore show that the co-finite typing premise can be instantiated with the concrete names chosen by the process transition. This is handled by auxiliary \emph{all fresh} lemmas, such as those for the \texttt{tensor} and \texttt{parr} prefix binders. Reformulating \texttt{ProcStep}$_\texttt{m}$ co-finitely would align the process and typing relations more directly and could reduce the need for these freshness-bridging lemmas, although its effect on the erasure simulation proofs remains to be checked.

\paragraph{Summary of shifted proof obligations}
The proof effort exposed by the mechanisation is concentrated less in transcribing the paper rules than in establishing the infrastructure required to use those rules inductively. This shift arises from deliberate choices in the representation and semantic architecture of the formalisation. The locally nameless representation avoids reasoning directly up to $\alpha$-equivalence, co-finite typing rules avoid fixing particular fresh representatives, and list-based environments provide a simple account of structural composition. These choices are accompanied by proof obligations concerning opening, freshness transfer, permutation, and reconstruction. These trade-offs show that mechanisation does not merely replace informal arguments with automation. Instead, it makes explicit the auxiliary infrastructure required by the chosen representation and semantic architecture. A separate limitation, considered next, is that the formalisation does not currently include the process-level structural congruence.

\subsection{Structural congruence}
The current formalisation does not include a structural congruence relation for processes. In the paper presentation of \pill{}, structural congruence identifies processes modulo the commutative, associative, and unit laws of parallel composition, together with scope manipulation for restriction. The mechanisation recovers the typing-level counterpart of the parallel-composition laws through the monoidal laws for hyperenvironments and the exchange rules of the typing relation. However, these results do not establish the corresponding equivalences between process terms. For example, while an empty hyperenvironment component can be removed from the typing structure, the formalisation does not identify $P \pp_p \nil$ with $P$ at the process level.

This omission matters because the operational semantics is defined over concrete process syntax rather than over processes modulo structural congruence. Although the transition rules propagate actions through parallel contexts, they do not identify syntactically different but structurally equivalent process terms. Consequently, intermediate processes may retain parallel units or a particular association and ordering of parallel components even when these distinctions are immaterial in the paper presentation. More substantially, scope-manipulation principles for restriction are currently unavailable. A full structural congruence development could therefore include defining an independent congruence relation and proving that typing and transitions are compatible with it. An alternative would be to incorporate symmetry, reassociation, or scope-management principles directly into the transition system. The current development has not shown signs of requiring either extension for the proofs established so far, but the omission limits the correspondence between the mechanised process semantics and the structural flexibility of the paper presentation.

\subsection{Current limitations}
The static formalisation covers the full \pill{} calculus, while the operational semantics and metatheory currently target \pimll{}. Within this fragment, the only incomplete case of \path{typability_subject_reduction}$_{\texttt{m}}$ is \texttt{res}. As previously discussed, this case requires recovering the post-transition hyperenvironment components carrying the restricted dual endpoints so that the enclosing \texttt{cut} derivation can be reconstructed. The current proof attempt reduces this remaining work to stronger component-preservation results for typed transitions, including single-endpoint tracking and paired-endpoint reconstruction (see \cref{lst:app-block-pres}). The completed \path{tensor_parr} extraction lemma in \cref{lst:app-extraction} illustrates the same permutation-sensitive form of component reconstruction for an internal communication case.

Several results from the paper metatheory also remain unmechanised, including the behavioural theory of bisimilarity and congruence, the diamond property, serialisation, non-interference, and readiness. The following section discusses these extensions, together with the remaining operational and proof-theoretic work needed to extend the current formalisation.
