\section{Background}\label{sec:background} % 2
This section begins by introducing the theoretical foundations underlying the formalisation following the presentation of~\citet{abs-2106-11818}, We cover the full \pill{} calculus, including processes, types, typing, environments / hyperenvironments, and judgements, then restrict attention to the multiplicative fragment \pimll{} when discussing operational semantics.

Following~\citet{abs-2106-11818}, we use \hl{$\thl{blue}$} to highlight types and \hl{$\phl{red}$} for process terms throughout this thesis.

% subsection 2.1
\subsection{Classical Linear Logic, CLL}\label{subsec:CLL}
Linear logic~\citep{G87} is a refinement of classical and intuitionistic logic. In contrast to these systems, where formulas can be freely duplicated or discarded, linear logic restricts structural rules such as weakening and contraction, and instead treats formulas as consumable resources. This yields a resource-sensitive interpretation of logic, where each assumption must be used in a controlled manner~\cite{sep-linear-logic}. In this thesis, we work in the classical presentation of linear logic, where sequents are symmetric and duality replaces the distinction between assumptions and conclusions.

In particular, conjunction and disjunction split into multiplicative and additive variants. The multiplicative fragment forms the core of the system and includes the tensor connective {$\thl{A \tensor B}$} and its dual {$\thl{A \parr B}$}, together with the corresponding units {$\thl{1}$} and {$\thl{\bot}$}. These connectives capture composition and interaction of resources.

Intuitively, {$\thl{A \tensor{} B}$} describes the joint availability of two independent resources, while {$\thl{A \parr{} B}$} captures their dual form of interaction. Under a process interpretation, {$\thl{\tensor{}}$} can be read as producing a value of type {$\thl{A}$} and continuing as {$\thl{B}$}, whereas {$\thl{A \parr{} B}$} corresponds to receiving a value of type {$\thl{A}$} and proceeding as {$\thl{B}$}. The units {$\thl{1}$} and {$\thl{\bot{}}$} represent the absence of further output and input, respectively. A simplified presentation of these rules is given below:
\begin{highlight}
\begin{figure}[h]
  \[
  \infer[\rlabel{\rname{$1$}}{rule:ll-one}]
    {\,\vdash \thl{1}\,}{}
  \qquad\qquad
  \infer[\rlabel{\rname{$\bot$}}{rule:ll-bot}]
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}, \thl{\bot}\,}
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}\,}
  \qquad\qquad
  \infer[\rlabel{\rname{$\tensor$}}{rule:ll-tensor}]
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}, \thl{\Delta}, \thl{A\tensor B}\,}
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}, \thl{A} \quad \vdash \thl{\Delta}, \thl{B}\,}
  \qquad\qquad
  \infer[\rlabel{\rname{$\parr$}}{rule:ll-parr}]
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}, \thl{A \parr B}\,}
    {\,\vdash \thl{\Gamma}, \thl{A}, \thl{B}\,}
  \]
\end{figure}
\end{highlight} 

Here \cref{rule:ll-tensor} presents a simplified form of the tensor rule from classical linear logic. In general, the tensor connective {$\thl{A \tensor B}$} combines two independent derivations. It requires a proof of {$\thl{A}$} and a proof of {$\thl{B}$}, each obtained from disjoint contexts, and produces a proof of the composite resource {$\thl{A \tensor B}$}. Intuitively, this corresponds to combining two independent resources into a single composite resource, and can be read  as producing a value of type {$\thl{A}$} and then continuing as {$\thl{B}$}.

The unit rule \cref{rule:ll-one} introduces the unit {$\thl{1}$}, which represents the absence of further obligations. It can be understood as an “empty output”, corresponding to a computation that performs no further interaction, and serves as the neutral element of tensor.

Dually, \cref{rule:ll-parr,rule:ll-bot} describe the behavior of the connective {$\thl{A \parr B}$} and its unit {$\thl{\bot}$}. The {$\thl{\parr}$} rule combines resources within a single context, reflecting a form of interaction where both components are made available to the surrounding context. From an operational perspective, this corresponds to interacting with an input of type {$\thl{A}$} while continuing as {$\thl{B}$}. The unit {$\thl{\bot}$} represents the absence of further input, acting as an “empty input” and serving as the dual neutral element. 

For example, two independent instances of the unit {$\thl{1}$} can be introduced by the \cref{rule:ll-one} and combined via \cref{rule:ll-tensor} to derive {$\thl{1 \tensor 1}$}:
\begin{highlight}
\[
  \infer[\tensor]
    {\,\vdash \thl{1 \tensor 1}\,}
    {
      \infer[\textit{$1$}]{\,\vdash \thl{1}\,}{}
      \quad
      \infer[\textsc{1}]{\,\vdash \thl{1}\,}{}
    }
\]
\end{highlight}

Here each leaf introduces one resource independently, and the tensor rule combines them into a single compound resource {$\thl{1 \tensor 1}$}. After this step, the individual resources are no longer available separately, reflecting the resource-sensitive nature of the system.

This resource-oriented view of logic provides the foundation for its applications in computer science, particularly in the study of concurrency and session-typed communication. In the following sections, this perspective is used to motivate the process interpretation underlying \pill.

\subsection{The \texorpdfstring{$\pi$}{pi}-Calculus: Processes and Communication}\label{subsec:processes}
The $\pi$-calculus~\citep{MPW92} is a process calculus for modeling concurrent computation with dynamic communication topology, where channel names themselves can be transmitted, allowing the communication network to evolve during execution. We follow the presentation of~\citet{abs-2106-11818}, which adopts~\citeauthor{W14}'s convention of using square brackets '$[\text{-}]$' for output and round parentheses '$(\text{-})$' for input.

Programs in \pimll{} are processes \hl{($\phl{P}$, $\phl{Q}$, $\phl{R}$, $\dots$)}, which communicate over names \hl{($\phl{x}$, $\phl{y}$, $\phl{z}$, $\dots$)} representing session endpoints. Sessions are binary (they involve exactly two endpoints), a discipline enforced by the typing system introduced in~\ref{subsec:types}. We assume an infinite set of names with decidable equality. The process terms of \pimll{} are defined by the following grammar:

\label{tab:pill-proc-syntax}
\begin{bnftable}% Taken from \cite{abs-2106-11818} with minor adjustments
  {\phl{P}, \phl{Q} \Coloneqq{}}
  {\infer{\choice{x}{P}{Q}}{\clientdup{x}{x'}{P} \pp{} \send{x}{y}{\nil}}}
    \phl{P}, \phl{Q} \Coloneqq{}
    & \send{x}{y}{P}               & output $y$ on $x$ and continue as $P$\\
    \mid{} & \recv{x}{y}{P}       & input $y$ on $x$ and continue as $P$\\
    \mid{} & \close{x}{P}         & output (empty message) on $x$ and continue as $P$\\
    \mid{} & \wait{x}{P}          & input (empty message) on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \res{xy}{P}          & name restriction (cut) \\
    \mid{} & P \pp Q              & parallel composition \\
    \mid{} & \inl{x}{P}           & select left on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \inr{x}{P}           & select right on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \choice{x}{P}{Q}     & offer a binary choice between $P$ or $Q$ on $x$ \\
    \mid{} & \sendtype{x}{A}{P}   & output type $A$ on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \recvtype{x}{X}{P}   & input a type as $X$ on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \server{x}{z_1,\dots,z_n}{P} & server (replicable process) on $x$ depending on servers on $z_1$ \dots $z_n$ \\
    \mid{} & \clientuse{x}{P}     & consume a server on $x$ and continue as $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \clientdup{x}{x'}{P} & duplicate server $x$ as $x'$ in $P$ \\
    \mid{} & \clientdisp{x}{P}    & dispose of a server on $x $\\
    \mid{} & \forward{x}{y}       & link $x$ and $y$ \\
    \mid{} & \nil{}               & terminated process
\end{bnftable}

Term {$\phl{\send{x}{y}{P}}$} allocates a fresh name {$\phl{y}$}, outputs it over {$\phl{x}$}, and proceeds as {$\phl{P}$}. Dually, {$\phl{\recv{x}{y}{P}}$} inputs a name over {$\phl{x}$}, binding it to {$\phl{y}$} in {$\phl{P}$}. Terms {$\phl{\close{x}{P}}$} and {$\phl{\wait{x}{P}}$} model empty output and input. Restriction {$\phl{\res{xy}{P}}$} connects the two endpoints {$\phl{x}$} and {$\phl{y}$} of {$\phl{P}$} to form a session, where the order of {$\phl{x}$} and {$\phl{y}$} is irrelevant and {$\phl{\res{xy}{P}} = \phl{\res{yx}{P}}$}. Parallel composition {$\phl{P \pp Q}$} runs {$\phl{P}$} and {$\phl{Q}$} concurrently, and {$\phl{\nil}$} is the terminated process, acting as the unit of parallel composition.

\begin{example}\label{ex:cut-unit-proc}
  Consider the process {$\phl{P} \defeq \phl{\res{xz}{( \send{x}{}{\nil} \pp \recv{z}{}{\send{y}{}{\nil}})}}$}. This process creates a private session between the restricted endpoints {$\phl{x}$} and {$\phl{z}$}. The left process, {$\phl{\send{x}{}{\nil}}$}, signals termination on {$\phl{x}$} before terminating, while the right process, {$\phl{\recv{z}{}{\send{y}{}{\nil}}}$}, waits for a termination signal on {$\phl{z}$} before proceeding by sending a termination signal on {$\phl{y}$} and then terminating.
\end{example}

This calculus provides the operational foundation for \pill. In the following section, we introduce the type system that governs how names are used, ensuring that each session endpoint is used exactly according to its protocol.

\subsection{Types and Propositions-as-Sessions Correspondence}\label{subsec:types}
In \pill, session types \hl{$(\thl{A}, \thl{B}, \thl{C}, \dots)$} are linear logic propositions~\citep{CP10, W14, abs-2106-11818}. Each type describes the communication protocol of a channel endpoint, and duality, the key structural feature of classical linear logic, ensures that the two endpoints of every session implement complementary protocols.

The correspondence between linear logic and session types used in this thesis follows the presentation of \citet{abs-2106-11818}, which builds on the accounts of \citet{CLMSW16} and \citet{W14}, is captured directly by the type grammar. Each connective denotes a communication action, where {$\thl{A \tensor B}$} describes sending a value of type {$\thl{A}$} and continuing as {$\thl{B}$}, while its dual {$\thl{A \parr B}$} describes receiving. The units {$\thl{1}$ and $\thl{\bot}$}, again, represent the absence of out and input, respectively. The additive connectives {$\thl{A \oplus B}$} and {$\thl{A \with B}$} capture choice in the sense of selection and offering of alternatives, while the exponentials {$\thl{\bang A}$} and {$\thl{\query A}$} govern replication. The {$\thl{\forall X.A}$} and {$\thl{\exists X.A}$} allow polymorphic session behavior. The type grammar of \pill{} is defined as follows:

\begin{bnftable2col}[\thl] % Taken from \cite{abs-2106-11818} with minor adjustments
    {\thl{A},\thl{B} \Coloneqq {}}
    {A \tensor{} B}{A \tensor{} B}
    \thl{A},\thl{B} \Coloneqq{} {} & A \tensor{} B & send $A$, proceed as $B$ &
    \mid{} {} & A \parr{B} & receive $A$, proceed as $B$ \\
    \mid{} {} & \one{} & empty output, unit for $\tensor$ &
    \mid{} {} & \bot{} & empty input, unit for $\parr$ \\
    \mid{} {} & {A \with{} B} & offer $A$ or $B$ &
    \mid{} {} & {A \oplus{} B} & select $A$ or $B$ \\
    \mid{} {} & {\exists{} X.A} & existential, type output &
    \mid{} {} & {\forall{} X.A} & universal, type input\\
    \mid{} {} & {\query{} A} & client request &
    \mid{} {} & {\bang{} A} & server accept\\
    \mid{} {} & {X} & type variable &
    \mid{} {} & {\dual{X}} & dual of type variable\\
\end{bnftable2col}

Duality is defined structurally on session types by exchanging each connective with its dual and forms an involution, i.e.\ {$\thl{{(A^\bot)}^\bot} = \thl{A}$}. Consequently, the endpoints of a well-typed session are always assigned dual types, ensuring they are compatibility:

\begin{highlight} % Taken from \cite{abs-2106-11818} with minor adjusments
\begin{gather*}

    \qquad
    \thl{\one^\bot} = \thl{\bot}
    \qquad
    \thl{(A \oplus B)}\thl{^\bot} = \thl{A^\bot \with B^\bot}
    \\
    \thl{(\bang A)}\thl{^\bot} = \thl{\query A^\bot}
    \qquad
    \thl{(\exists X.A)}\thl{^\bot} = \thl{\forall X.A^\bot}
    \qquad
    \thl{{(X)}^\bot} = \thl{X^\bot}
\end{gather*}
\end{highlight}

\subsection{Environments, Hyperenvironments and Judgements}\label{subsec:env-hyper-judge}
Typing in \pill{} is organised into two layers. Environments track how individual names are typed, while hyperenvironments track which names are used independently in parallel.

Following the presentation in \citet{abs-2106-11818}, \emph{environments}~(\hl{$\thl{\Gamma}$, $\thl{\Delta}$, $\thl{\Xi}$, \dots}) are defined as lists allowing exchange, meaning order is irrelevant. They can be written as {$\thl{\Gamma} = \phl{x_1}:\thl{A}, \dots, \phl{x_n}:\thl{A_n}$}, where each {$\phl{x_i}$} is associated to its respective {$\thl{A_i}$}, for $1\leq i \leq n$. Environments can be merged as long as they do not share any names for example assume {$\thl{\Gamma}$} and {$\thl{\Delta}$} do not share any names, then {$\thl{\Gamma, \Delta}$} is the environment containing exactly the associations in {$\thl{\Gamma}$} and {$\thl{\Delta}$} regardless of ordering. Environments act as a partial commutative monoid, using ``$,$'' as the sum and the empty environment {$\thl{\emptyseq}$} as unit:

\begin{highlight} % Taken directly from \cite{abs-2106-11818}
\[
\thl{\Gamma, \emptyseq} = \thl{\Gamma}
\qquad
\thl{\Gamma, \Delta} = \thl{\Delta, \Gamma}
\qquad
\thl{(\Gamma, \Delta), \Xi} = \thl{\Gamma, (\Delta , \Xi)}
\]
\end{highlight}

Environments dictate how names are used, but it does not distinguish whether names are used independently or in parallel. That role is handled by  handled by \emph{hyperenvironments} (\hl{$\thl{\hyp{G}}$, $\thl{\hyp{H}}$, $\thl{\hyp{I}}$, \dots}), which are collections of environments joined using the parallel operator `$\pp$'. They can be written as follows {$\thl{\hyp{G}} = \thl{\Gamma_1,\dots, \Gamma_n}$}, where {$\thl{\Gamma_1,\dots, \Gamma_n}$} is a collection of environments. Given {$\thl{\hyp{G}}$} and {$\thl{\hyp{H}}$} having no names in common then {$\thl{\hyp{G} \pp \hyp{H}}$} is the hyperenvironment containing exactly the environments of {$\thl{\hyp{G}}$} and {$\thl{\hyp{H}}$}, ignoring order. Like environments, all names in a hyperenvironment are unique, they can be combined as long as they do not share any names, and they act as a partial commutative monoid using '$\pp$' the sum and $\emptyhyp$ as unit, where $\emptyhyp$ is the hyperenvironment containing no environments:

\begin{highlight} % Taken directly from \cite{abs-2106-11818}
\[
  \thl{\hyp G \pp \emptyhyp} = \thl{\hyp G}
  \qquad
  \thl{\hyp G \pp \hyp H} = \thl{\hyp H \pp \hyp G}
  \qquad
  \thl{(\hyp G \pp \hyp H) \pp \hyp I} = \thl{\hyp G \pp (\hyp H \pp \hyp I)}
\]
\end{highlight}

\emph{Judgements}, written as {$\judge{}{P}{\hyp{G}}$}, states that the process {$\phl{P}$} uses its free names in accordance with {$\thl{\hyp G}$} and can be derived using the inference rules displayed in \cref{fig:pill-typing-rules}.

\emph{Typing Derivations} $(\der{D}, \der{E}, \der{F}, \dots)$ are written as {$\deduce{\judge{}{P}{\hyp G}}{\der D}$} and are read as the derivation $\der{D}$ having the judgement {$\judge{}{P}{\hyp{G}}$} as its conclusion. The meaning of this statement is that the process, {$\phl{P}$}, appearing in the judgement concluding a derivation is well-typed.

The multiplicative rules form the core of the system. \Cref{rule:pill-cut} composes two processes in {$\phl{P}$} sharing dual endpoints {$\cht{x}{A}$} and {$\cht{y}{A^\bot}$}, hiding them via restriction. This is the logical equivalent of communication. \Cref{rule:pill-mix} and \cref{rule:pill-mix-0} compose independent processes in parallel and introduce the terminated process, respectively.

\Cref{rule:pill-tensor} and \cref{rule:pill-parr} type output and input. Note that \cref{rule:pill-tensor} types the sent name {$\phl{y}$} in a \emph{separate} environment from the continuation, reflecting that the two are independent resources. By contrast, \cref{rule:pill-parr} keeps {$\phl{y}$} and the continuation {$\phl{x}$} in the \emph{same} environment, reflecting their sequential dependency. The units \cref{rule:pill-one} and \cref{rule:pill-bot} type empty output and empty input, where \cref{rule:pill-one} requires the continuation to be well-typed in the empty hyperenvironment, meaning {$\phl{P}$} is necessarily semantically equivalent to $\nil$.

The additive rules (\cref{rule:pill-oplus-1}, \cref{rule:pill-oplus-2}, and \cref{rule:pill-with}) type selection and offering of alternative processing paths. Note that \cref{rule:pill-with} requires both branches to be typed in the \emph{same} context {$\thl{\Gamma}$}, reflecting that the choice of branch is made by the other endpoint.

The exponential rules type server replication. The \cref{rule:pill-!} requires all free names except {$\phl{x}$} to be typed using {$\thl{\query}$}, enforcing that a server only depends on other servers. The \cref{rule:pill-?}, \cref{rule:pill-weaken}, and \cref{rule:pill-contract} allow a client to use, discard, or duplicate a server respectively.

The \cref{rule:pill-axiom} types a link between two dual endpoints, \hl{$\phl{\forward{x}{y}}$}, forwarding all messages sent on {$\phl{x}$} safely to {$\phl{y}$} and vice versa. \Cref{rule:pill-exists} and \cref{rule:pill-forall} type polymorphic communication, where the side condition {$\thl{X \notin \ft(\Gamma)}$} in \cref{rule:pill-forall}, ensures the introduced type variable, {$\thl{X}$}, does not accendentally shadow one already existing in {$\thl{\Gamma}$}.

\begin{example}\label{ex:cut-unit-der}
  \begin{highlight}
    The process from~\Cref{ex:cut-unit-proc} has the typing $\judge{}{\res{zx}{(\recv{z}{}{\send{y}{}{\nil}} \pp \send{x}{}{\nil})}}{\cht{y}{1}}$, as shown by the derivation below:
    \[
      \infer[\ref{rule:pill-cut}]
        {\judge{}{\res{xz}{(\send{x}{}{\nil} \pp \recv{z}{}{\send{y}{}{\nil}})}}{\cht{y}{1}}}
          {\infer[\ref{rule:pill-mix}]

            {\judge{}{\recv{z}{}{\send{y}{}{\nil}}}{\cht{y}{1}, \cht{z}  {\bot}}}

Note that, for the typing envrionment {$\thl{\cht{x}{1} \pp \cht{y}{1}, \cht{z}{\bot}}$} to align with the premise of~\cref{rule:pill-cut}, we utilise the monoidal properties of environments and hyperenvironments. In particular, using their identity laws, we instantiate the meta-variables of the rule as {$\thl{\hyp{G}} = \thl{\emptyhyp}$} and {$\thl{\Gamma} = \thl{\emptyseq}$}. Under these instantiations, the premise of~\cref{rule:pill-cut}: {$\judge{}{P}{\hyp{G} \pp \Gamma, \cht{x}{A} \pp \Delta, \cht{y}{\dual{A}}}$} can be viewed as {$\thl{\cht{x}{A} \pp \Delta, \cht{y}{\dual{A}}}$}. This effectively instantiates the~\ref{rule:pill-cut} over the dual types {$\thl{A} = \thl{1}$} and {$\thl{\dual{A}} = \thl{\bot}$} on channels {$\phl{x}$} and {$\phl{y}$}, with {$\thl{\Gamma} = \cht{y}{1}$}, while ensuring the restricted endpoints implement dual session behaviours, fulfilling the requirements for creating a valid~\ref{rule:pill-cut} (session).

The operational semantics rely explicitly on the structural equivalence (\cref{rule:pill-alpha}) to rename restricted variables via $\alpha$-equivalence prior to a transition. In paper-based presentations, $\alpha$-equivalence is often handled implicitly through Barendregt's Variable Convention. In a mechanised setting, however, reasoning over named variables requires freshness conditions, capture-avoiding substitution, and explicit renaming lemmas, introducing substantial proof overhead \citep{A05,C12}. As discussed in \cref{sec:binding-architecture}, this motivates the use of a different binding representation for the syntax of \pill{}, one designed to reduce explicit $\alpha$-equivalence reasoning and simplify the treatment of bound variables.

Actions are defined in the set $\ActSet = \{\lclose{x}, \lwait{x}, \lsend{x}{y}, \lrecv{x}{y} \mid x, y \text{ names}\}$, representing empty output, empty input, name output, and name input respectively. These come together with the silent label $\tau$ for internal communication and the parallel composition of labels $l \mid l'$ to form the set of transition labels, defined as $\LblSet = \{\tau, l, (\lsync{l}{l'}) \mid l, l' \in \ActSet, \is(l) \cap \is(l') = \emptyset\}$. The restriction imposed on the parallel composition of labels exists to ensure two labels do not introduce the same name, which would break linear resource management.

The free and introduced names of labels are defined by $\fs(x[]) = \fs(x()) = \fs(x[y]) = \fs(x(y)) = \{x\}$, $\fs(\tau) = \is(\tau) = \emptyset$, and $\fs(l \mid l') = \fs(l) \cup \fs(l')$, and introduced names by $\is(x[]) = \is(x()) = \emptyset$, $\is(x[y]) = \is(x(y)) = \{y\}$, and $\is(l \mid l') = \is(l) \cup \is(l')$. The parallel label $l \mid l'$ additionally requires $\is(l) \cap \is(l') = \emptyset$, ensuring no two components introduce the same name.

Each logical rule (\ref{rule:pill-one},~\ref{rule:pill-bot},~\ref{rule:pill-tensor},~\ref{rule:pill-parr}) has a corresponding transition axiom. These follow the prefix-continuation pattern {$\phl{\prefixed{\pi}{P}} \lto{\pi} \phl{P}$}, where performing the action $\pi$ leads to the remainder of the process. \Cref{rule:pill-mix-sync} allows for the pairing of concurrent actions via $\lsync{l}{l'}$, which is used to facilitate communication by letting two endpoints connected by a restriction perform compatible actions. When two compatible actions are performed over endpoints connected by a restriction, \cref{rule:pill-cut-one,rule:pill-cut-tensor} simplify the term into an internal $\tau$-transition, modeling the synchronisation of the session.

\subsection{Process Semantics and Erasure}\label{subsec:process-semantics-erasure}
While the typing derivations of \pill{} dictate the valid interactions within a session, the underlying process terms possess a standard, untyped operational behavior that closely mirrors the classic $\pi$-calculus. To formally relate the typed derivations to their untyped execution, we follow \citet{abs-2106-11818} and define an erasure function, $\mathit{proc} : \DerSet \to \mathit{Proc}$, which strips away the typing information from a derivation. Recursively, $\mathit{proc}$ maps the head of a typing derivation to the specific process constructor it introduces, continuing structurally onto the process's continuation. For any well-typed \pill{} term, $proc(\hyp{D})$ effectively extracts the pure process term from the derivation's conclusion.

The semantics for process terms must inherently agree with the semantics of derivations for well-typed terms. As formulated by~\citet{abs-2106-11818}, this relationship is established functorially. Let $\mathcal{P}(X) = \{X' \mid X' \subseteq X\}$ and $\mathcal{L}(X) = X \times \LblSet$ be endofunctors representing the states and labeled transitions, respectively. The erasure function $proc$ acts as a homomorphism from LTS of derivations ($\mlts{d}$) to the LTS of processes ($\mlts{p}$), ensuring that the square of these LTS mappings commutes.

In the context of operational semantics, this functorial homomorphism yields an Erasure theorem. It guarantees that the Labeled Transition System for derivations and the SOS for untyped processes simulate each other exactly:

\begin{theorem}[Erasure]\label{thm:operational-correspondence}
The process LTS ($\mlts{p}$) enjoys erasure with respect to the derivation LTS ($\mlts{d}$). Concretely, for any valid typing derivation $\der{D}$ and label $l \in \LblSet$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Soundness of Erasure:} If $\der{D} \lto{l} \der{D}'$, then $\mathit{proc}(\der{D}) \lto{l} \mathit{proc}(\der{D}')$.
    \item \textbf{Completeness of Erasure:} If $\mathit{proc}(\der{D}) \lto{l} P'$, then there exists a derivation $\der{D}'$ such that $\der{D} \lto{l} \der{D}'$ and $\mathit{proc}(\der{D}') = P'$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}[Typability Preservation]\label{cor:typability-preservation}
If $P$ is well-typed and $P \lto{l} P'$, then $P'$ is well-typed.
\end{corollary}

This theorem allows us to define the operational target for \pill{} processes without the syntactic overhead of typing environments. Using the erasure formulation, we can transform the operational semantics for derivations directly into an SOS specification for processes.

The corresponding evolution of these typing resources is described by the environment semantics introduced next.

\subsection{Semantics of Typing Environments}\label{subsec:semantics-environments}
Just as processes evolve through computation, the typing environments that govern them must also evolve to accurately track the state of a session. In \pill, types capture communication protocols. Consequently, the transition rules for typing environments specify exactly which actions are permitted and how the available resources are consumed by those actions. 

Similar to how we defined the process erasure function, we again follow \citet{abs-2106-11818} and define a function $env : \DerSet \to \mathit{Env}$ that maps a valid typing derivation directly to the hyperenvironment in its conclusion (i.e., {$env(\judge{}{P}{\hyp{G}}) = \thl{\hyp{G}}$}). Using this extraction,~\citet{abs-2106-11818} derive a LTS specifically for typing environments ($\mlts{e}$), shown in~\cref{fig:mpill-sos-types}.

In this LTS, observable actions actively consume resources from the environment. For example, if a hyperenvironment contains a waiting channel {$\cht{x}{\bot}$}, performing an input action $\lwait{x}$ will consume this type, removing it from the resulting environment. Conversely, internal communications do not alter the available external resources. This is formalised by the $\tau$-reflexivity axiom, {$\thl{\hyp{G} \mid \Gamma} \lto{\tau} \thl{\hyp{G} \mid \Gamma}$}, which states that any valid hyperenvironment transitions to itself under a silent $\tau$-action.

The rules in \cref{fig:mpill-sos-types} define which transitions are available at the environment level. For a well-typed process, however, process behaviour and resource evolution must step in unison such that whenever the process performs an action, its typing environment must be able to perform the corresponding transition. This correspondence is the property of \textbf{session fidelity}. Just as the $\mathit{proc}$ projection extracts the process behaviour of a typed transition, the $\mathit{env}$ projection extracts its corresponding environment evolution.

\begin{theorem}[Session Fidelity]\label{thm:session-fidelity}
Let $\der{D}$ be a valid typing derivation such that $env(\der{D}) = {\thl{\hyp{G}}}$ and {$proc(\der{D}) = \phl{P}$}. If the process transitions such that {$\phl{P} \lto{l} \phl{P'}$}, then the environment can mimic this transition {$\thl{\hyp{G}} \lto{l} \thl{\hyp{G}'}$}, and there exists a new valid derivation $\der{D}'$ for {$\phl{P'}$} under {$\thl{\hyp{G}'}$}.
\end{theorem}

To illustrate Session Fidelity in action, we can observe how the environment is constrained to follow the exact execution traces of the process it types.

\begin{example}[Session Fidelity in Execution]
Recall the process derivation from \cref{ex:expanded-cut-unit-execution}, initially typed by the hyperenvironment $\thl{\hyp{G}} = \cht{v}{1}, \cht{w}{\bot} \pp \cht{y}{1}$. The process first performs an observable input action on $w$. By session fidelity, this process transition is mirrored by the corresponding environment transition, consuming the resource typed by $\bot$:
\begin{highlight}
  \[
    \begin{aligned}
      \cht{v}{1}, \cht{w}{\bot} \pp \cht{y}{1}
      &\lto{\lwait{w}} \cht{v}{1} \pp \cht{y}{1} \\
      &\lto{\tau}       \cht{v}{1} \pp \cht{y}{1} \\
      &\lto{\lsend{y}{}} \cht{v}{1}.
    \end{aligned}
  \]
\end{highlight}
After the first transition, the process derivative is typed by $\cht{v}{1} \pp \cht{y}{1}$. Viewed in isolation, the environment transition system admits an output action on $y$, since $\cht{y}{1}$ is available as a resource. However, session fidelity does not state that every environment transition can be realised by the current process. Rather it states that each process transition has a corresponding environment transition. In the process term, the action $\lsend{y}{}$ remains guarded until an internal synchronisation has taken place. The environment therefore mirrors the intermediate $\tau$-transition without changing its resources, and only afterwards mirrors the observable output on $y$ by consuming the final $\cht{y}{1}$ component.
\end{example}

Session fidelity is a central correctness property of a session-typed calculus: it expresses that process evolution is matched by the evolution of its typing resources. In the paper presentation, this correspondence is supported by implicit conventions for fresh names, bound variables, and structural rearrangement. In a proof assistant, these conventions must instead be represented explicitly and manipulated by proved lemmas. Establishing that types are consumed correctly therefore requires a concrete treatment of binding, opening, substitution, and freshness. The next chapter develops this binding architecture, which provides the foundation for the subsequent Lean formalisation and its metatheory.

\subsection{Managing Bound Variables}\label{subsec:burden-of-bound-variables}
Having established the operational semantics of \pill{} and the ultimate goal of verifying Session Fidelity, we must address the treatment of bound variables and freshness, a central technical challenge that underpins the entire formalisation.

As we have seen, many constructs in \pill{} introduce scoped identifiers. Restriction binds pairs of session endpoints, input constructs bind received channel names, polymorphic communication binds type variables, and duplication constructs introduce fresh channel endpoints. These bindings appear throughout both the process syntax and the labelled transition system.

The concrete choice of bound and introduced names is semantically irrelevant provided the binding structure is preserved. For example, renaming a restricted channel does not change the behaviour of a process. However, reasoning formally about substitutions, transitions, and typing derivations requires these names, scopes, and freshness conditions to be tracked explicitly.

This principle is formalised as \emph{$\alpha$-equivalence}, which identifies terms that differ only in the names of their bound variables. For instance, the following two processes are $\alpha$-equivalent:
\begin{highlight}
%
\[
\phl{\res{ab}{(\send{a}{}{\nil} \pp \recv{b}{}{\send{x}{}{\nil}})}}
\aleq
\phl{\res{zw}{(\send{z}{}{\nil} \pp \recv{w}{}{\send{x}{}{\nil}})}}
\]
%
\end{highlight}
The operational semantics of \pill{} relies on $\alpha$-equivalence using \cref{rule:pill-alpha} to rename bound variables on the fly, ensuring that restricted scopes and input prefixes do not accidentally capture free names as processes evolve.

\begin{highlight}
Substitution raises this exact issue. Consider the substitution where the free name $x$ is replaced by $z$ in the right-hand process from the previous example:
%
\[
\phl{\res{zw}{(\send{z}{}{\nil} \pp \recv{w}{}{\send{x}{}{\nil}})}} 
\]
%
Here $z$ is already bound by the restriction, so naively replacing $x$ with $z$ would cause the restriction to capture the newly inserted $z$. Before the substitution can proceed, and because restriction binds session endpoints as pairs, both the bound $z$ and $w$ must be $\alpha$-renamed to fresh names, such as to $a$ and $b$. Similarly, any binder within the process that would capture the substituted name must also be renamed before substitution continues.

In paper-based presentations this is commonly handled by \emph{Barendregt's variable convention}, which assumes bound variables are always chosen distinct from free variables and one another whenever convenient. This makes proofs readable but remains an informal meta-level assumption.
\end{highlight}

A proof assistant requires every freshness condition and renaming step to be represented and verified explicitly~\cite{A05}. A direct mechanisation using named variables therefore introduces substantial proof overhead for capture avoidance and $\alpha$-equivalence reasoning. To avoid this, the formalisation adopts a \emph{locally nameless} representation of binding, which eliminates $\alpha$-equivalence as a proof obligation by construction. This is the subject of the following chapter.
